:::hook
Several of India's most admired consumer companies grew profits steadily for years, yet investors who bought at peak excitement waited a long time for decent returns. **The business was great. The price was the problem.**
:::

## Where returns come from

Over long periods:

**Share return ≈ EPS growth + dividend yield ± change in P/E**

Because **price = P/E × EPS**, a share can rise because profits grew, because investors became willing to pay a higher multiple, or both. It can fall even while profits grow, if the multiple shrinks.

:::example
Two investors buy the same company, which grows EPS **12% a year for 5 years** (EPS × 1.76).

| | P/E when bought | P/E after 5 yrs | Price change | Yearly return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Investor A (bought at peak hype) | 40 | 25 | × 1.10 | **≈ 2%** |
| Investor B (bought when ignored) | 15 | 20 | × 2.35 | **≈ 18.6%** |

Same business, same growth. **The starting price alone separated about 2% a year from about 18.6% a year.**
:::

## Thinking about P/E sensibly

- **High P/E** = the market expects high growth for long. That's fine if it happens. If growth slows, both earnings expectations *and* the multiple fall: a double hit.
- **Low P/E** can mean a bargain, or a **value trap**: falling profits, heavy debt, governance problems, a dying industry.
- Compare P/E with the company's **own history**, **peers**, and the **index**, and always alongside growth, ROCE and debt (Module 9).

## Value, price and the margin of safety

A business's **value** is what its future cash flows are worth today. Nobody knows them exactly; estimates involve guesses about growth, margins and interest rates (Module 6: higher rates lower the value of future cash).

So careful investors demand a **margin of safety**: buy only when the price is well below their estimate of value, leaving room to be wrong.

:::trap
Stories sell shares: "India's next giant", "EV revolution", "AI play". Before buying any story, ask one question: **what growth is the current price already assuming, and how likely is that?** If the answer is "flawless growth for 15 years", the downside is large.
:::

:::dive Reverse-engineering a P/E
Turn the question around. If Deccan Chai trades at P/E 20, you're paying 20 years of today's profit. If you want, say, a 12% yearly return, profits have to grow fast enough to justify that price and still leave you 12%. Try writing down what growth you'd need for 10 years and whether that's realistic for a tea-café chain. This "what must be true" exercise is more useful to beginners than any formula.
:::
