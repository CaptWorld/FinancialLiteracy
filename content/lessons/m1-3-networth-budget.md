:::hook
Two people earn ₹1 lakh a month. One has ₹8 lakh saved. The other has a car loan, a phone on EMI, and ₹60,000 on credit cards. Same salary, very different lives. **Income is what comes in. Net worth is what stays.**
:::

## Net worth: your financial scorecard

**Net worth = assets − liabilities.**

- **Assets**: bank balances, FDs, mutual funds, stocks, EPF/PPF/NPS balances, gold (at realistic resale value), property (at a cautious market value).
- **Liabilities**: outstanding loan principal, credit card dues, money borrowed from friends/family.

Check it twice a year. The trend matters more than the number. At 27 with a good salary, a rising net worth each half-year means you're on track.

## Cash flow: the monthly engine

**Cash flow = take-home pay − all spending (including EMIs).** Everything else (emergency fund, investing, goals) is funded from positive cash flow. If it's thin, no investment product will fix it.

:::task
**Track one month.** Export last month's bank and card statements (or look at UPI history) and sort spending into: Rent & bills · Food & groceries · Transport · Family support · Shopping & fun · EMIs & card payments · Other. Enter totals in **Tools → Budget**. Don't judge yet; just measure.
:::

## A budget that actually survives

Rigid budgets fail. What works for most people:

1. **Pay yourself first.** Set up auto-transfers/SIPs for the day after salary credit. Savings happen before you can spend them.
2. **Cover fixed costs**: rent, bills, EMIs, family support.
3. **Spend the rest guilt-free.** If money's left at month-end, great. If you overspent, adjust the *next* month's spending, not the savings.

A common starting split is roughly needs / wants / savings, but your numbers depend on rent and family responsibilities. Start with any savings rate you can sustain and raise it with each salary hike.

:::trap
Lifestyle creep: every raise gets absorbed by a slightly bigger flat, more eating out, a costlier phone. Decide *before* the raise arrives what share of it goes to savings. You'll formalise this as a "step-up rule" later.
:::

## Emergency fund

Before investing for growth, build a buffer for job loss, a medical bill, or urgent help for family. A common guideline is **several months of essential expenses** (more if your job is less stable or family depends on you). Keep it:

- **Safe**: bank deposits (insured up to a limit by DICGC), liquid funds.
- **Fast**: usable within a day.
- **Separate**: a different account so you don't dip into it.

:::checklive dicgc
Check the current deposit insurance limit per depositor per bank. If your emergency fund exceeds it, consider splitting across two banks.
:::

## Saving vs investing

| | Saving | Investing |
|---|---|---|
| For | Near-term needs (< ~3 years), emergencies | Long-term goals (5+ years) |
| Where | Savings account, FD, liquid fund | Equity/debt mutual funds, EPF/PPF/NPS |
| Risk | Very low | Ups and downs, can lose in short term |
| Beats inflation? | Barely or not | Aims to, over long periods |

## The order of priorities

1. Small emergency fund (even one month helps)
2. Clear high-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans, loan apps)
3. Health insurance and, if anyone depends on you, term insurance (Module 5)
4. Full emergency fund
5. Invest for goals: start small, step up regularly
