:::hook
The bank RM was friendly and the brochure said "guaranteed". Arjun's cousin signed a plan paying ₹50,000 a year. Ten years later he wanted the money for a house. The surrender value was **less than he'd paid in**. Nobody had lied exactly. They just never showed him the real return.
:::

## The products

- **Endowment**: pay premiums for years; get a lump sum at maturity or on death.
- **Money-back**: like endowment, but pays chunks every few years.
- **"Guaranteed income / savings" plans**: pay for some years, get fixed payouts later.
- **ULIP (Unit Linked Insurance Plan)**: insurance + market-linked investment, with a 5-year lock-in and several charges.
- **Whole life**: cover to 99/100 with savings, costly.

All mix **protection** with **investment**. That's the problem: you get **small cover** for a **big premium**, at a **low return**, with **penalties to leave**.

## The real return: IRR

The honest measure is the **IRR**: the single yearly rate that matches what you pay with what you get, accounting for **when**.

:::example
**Plan:** pay ₹50,000/year for 15 years. Get ₹13,00,000 at the end of year 20. Life cover: ₹6.5 lakh.

- Total paid: ₹7.5 lakh. Received: ₹13 lakh. Looks like "almost double"!
- But the money waits up to 20 years. **IRR ≈ 4.2% a year**, roughly inflation, often less after it.

**Alternative, same ₹50,000 a year:**
- ₹1 crore term cover to age 60 (premium varies; say ~₹12,000/year, illustrative)
- ₹38,000/year into a safe, government-backed option at ~7% for 15 years, left for 5 more → **≈ ₹14.3 lakh at year 20**
- If invested in a diversified equity index fund instead (not guaranteed; could be higher or lower): at 10% a year → ≈ ₹21.4 lakh

| | Endowment | Term + safe investing |
|---|---|---|
| Life cover | ₹6.5 lakh | **₹1 crore** |
| Money at year 20 | ₹13 lakh | **≈ ₹14.3 lakh** (at 7%) |
| Flexibility | Penalties to exit | Can withdraw (within each product's rules) |
:::

## How it's sold, and what to say

| What you hear | What it often means |
|---|---|
| "Guaranteed returns" | Guaranteed **low** returns, ~4–6% |
| "Like an FD, but tax-free" | It's not a deposit. Long lock-in, surrender charges. Tax-free maturity also has conditions. |
| "Double your money" | In 15–20 years, which is about 4–5% a year |
| "Only for 5 years" | Pay for 5, locked in for 10–15 |
| "Offer ends today" | Pressure to skip reading |

**Say:** "Please share the benefit illustration and policy wording. I'll calculate the IRR and get back." A genuine product survives that question.

:::trap
Banks earn commissions on insurance. A bank relationship manager selling you a policy is a salesperson for that insurer, not an adviser working for you. If you were misled, cancel within the **30-day free-look period**, and complain to the insurer, then via IRDAI's Bima Bharosa system or the Insurance Ombudsman.
:::

## Already own one? Don't panic, calculate

1. Find the **surrender value** (what you'd get today) and the **paid-up value** (what you'd get at maturity if you stop paying).
2. Compare three paths:
   - **Continue**: calculate the IRR of the *remaining* premiums against the maturity value. Sometimes, late in a policy, continuing is fine.
   - **Make it paid-up**: stop paying, keep reduced benefits, invest new money better.
   - **Surrender**: take the money now and invest it better; often painful early on.
3. **Buy term cover first**, before stopping any policy, so you're never uncovered.
4. For large or complex policies, a **fee-only SEBI-registered investment adviser** (paid by you, not by commission) can run the numbers.

:::dive How to calculate IRR yourself
In Google Sheets or Excel, put one cash flow per row, each row one year apart. Premiums are negative: −50,000 in rows 1–15 (paid at the start of years 1–15). Put 0 in rows 16–20, and the maturity as positive +13,00,000 in row 21 (the end of year 20). Then use `=IRR(A1:A21)` and you'll get about 4.2%. Do this with the benefit illustration of any policy you're offered. The **XIRR** function handles exact dates and works for SIPs too (Module 7).
:::

:::dive Are ULIPs different?
Modern ULIPs have lower charges than old ones and invest in market funds. But you still pay mortality charges for small cover, fund choices are limited, there's a 5-year lock-in, and switching away is hard. A term plan plus a low-cost index fund (Module 7) usually does the same job more cheaply and flexibly.
:::
