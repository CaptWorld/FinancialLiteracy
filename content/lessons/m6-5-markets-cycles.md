:::hook
In 2020 India's economy shrank and millions of people lost work for months. Indian stock indices nevertheless finished the year **higher than they started**. If that sounds impossible, this lesson is for you: **markets price the future, not the present.**
:::

## Bonds: the see-saw

A **bond** pays fixed interest (the **coupon**) and returns its face value at maturity. When market interest rates change, the bond's coupon doesn't, so its **price** adjusts.

:::example
A 10-year bond, face value ₹1,000, paying 7% (₹70 a year).

| Market rates move to | Bond's price becomes |
|---|---|
| 8% (rates rise) | ≈ **₹933** (it now pays less than new bonds) |
| 7% (no change) | ₹1,000 |
| 6% (rates fall) | ≈ **₹1,074** (it now pays more than new bonds) |

Longer bonds move more; short ones barely move. That's why **debt mutual funds** holding long bonds can lose money when RBI raises rates, and gain when it cuts (Module 7).
:::

The **yield** is the return you'd get buying the bond at today's price and holding to maturity. "Bond yields rose" means prices fell.

## Shares: expectations, not news

A share's price reflects what investors expect the company to earn **in future**, and how much they're willing to pay for those earnings (more when rates are low and mood is good, less when rates are high or fear is high).

So markets often:

- **fall before** a slowdown shows in GDP data,
- **rise before** the economy recovers,
- **react to surprises**: "growth 6% vs 7% expected" can move prices more than the growth itself.

## The cycle, and what typically happens

| Phase | Economy | Often (not always) |
|---|---|---|
| Expansion | Growth and hiring strong | Shares do well; inflation may build |
| Overheating | Inflation rises | RBI raises rates → bonds fall, shares wobble |
| Slowdown / recession | Growth falls, layoffs | Shares fall early; RBI cuts → bonds rise |
| Recovery | Growth returns | Shares often rise well before data improves |

These are tendencies with many exceptions: oil shocks, wars, pandemics and policy surprises break the pattern. That's why **forecasting cycles to trade them doesn't work reliably**, even for professionals.

:::trap
"Sell now, recession coming!" videos get views because fear sells. To profit you must be right **twice**: when to get out and when to get back in. Many of the market's best days come right after its worst, and missing a handful of them can sharply cut long-term returns. Module 14 covers the behaviour behind this.
:::

## What this means for your plan

1. **Emergency fund and insurance first.** The real recession risk for a salaried person is losing income, not market prices.
2. **Match money to time**: money needed within ~3 years → safe options (FD, liquid/short-term debt); 5+ years → can include equity, accepting swings.
3. **Invest regularly (SIP)** regardless of headlines. Buying through cycles averages out the price.
4. **Read the economy to understand, not to trade.** It helps you judge loans, FD timing, job risk and sales pitches.

:::dive Why low interest rates push up asset prices
If FDs pay 4%, investors accept lower returns on shares, property and bonds, so they pay higher prices for them. When rates rise to 8%, those same assets must offer more, so prices fall. This "discount rate" effect is why rate cycles move almost every asset at once, and why periods of ultra-low rates (2020–21 globally) were followed by sharp falls in many assets when rates jumped (2022).
:::
