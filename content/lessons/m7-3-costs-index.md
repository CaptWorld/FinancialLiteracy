:::hook
Two neighbours invest ₹10,000 a month in **the same fund, with the same manager and the same shares**, for 25 years. One ends with about ₹24 lakh more. The only difference: one picked the **direct plan**.
:::

## The expense ratio

Every fund deducts its costs from the NAV each day. You never see a bill; your return is simply lower.

- **TER (Total Expense Ratio)**: the all-in yearly cost, as % of your money.
- From **April 2026**, SEBI requires funds to show the **base expense ratio (BER)** (the AMC's fee) separately from brokerage, regulatory charges and taxes, with TER as their total. Costs above SEBI's caps can't be charged to investors.
- Index funds and ETFs typically have the lowest TERs; actively managed small-cap and thematic funds among the highest.

## Direct vs regular

Every scheme has two plans:

- **Regular**: bought via a distributor (bank, agent, many apps). The TER includes their **commission**, every year, for as long as you hold.
- **Direct**: bought straight from the AMC, MF Central, or platforms offering direct plans. **No commission.** Lower TER.

:::example
₹10,000 a month for 25 years in the same fund:

| | Yearly return | Value after 25 years |
|---|---|---|
| Direct plan | 11.3% | ≈ ₹1.68 crore |
| Regular plan (0.9% more cost) | 10.4% | ≈ ₹1.43 crore |
| **Difference** | | **≈ ₹24 lakh** |

The returns are illustrative. The cost gap is real, and it compounds.
:::

## Active vs index: what the data says

An **active fund** pays a manager to pick shares to beat a benchmark. An **index fund** just holds the index.

**SPIVA India (data to 30 June 2026)**, the share of active funds that **underperformed** their benchmark:

| Category | 1 year | 5 years | 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large-cap | 79% | 85% | **74%** |
| ELSS | 60% | 71% | **80%** |
| Mid/small-cap | 15% | 55% | **82%** |
| Government bond | 78% | 91% | **75%** |
| Composite bond | 29% | 76% | **93%** |

Notice the mid/small-cap 1-year number: in some short periods many active funds win. Over longer periods, most lose. And **you can't reliably pick the winners in advance**, because last decade's winners rarely repeat.

**Why?** Before costs, all investors together earn the market's return. Active funds charge more and trade more, so after costs most of them fall behind. Index funds keep almost all of the market return.

## Choosing an index fund

1. **Pick the index**: a broad one (e.g. Nifty 50 or a broader large/mid index) as a core.
2. **Direct plan, growth option**.
3. **Low TER** and **low tracking error** (how closely it follows the index).
4. **Decent size and history** (AUM, a few years of track record).

Index **ETFs** do the same but trade on the stock exchange like shares, and need a demat account. Index funds are simpler for SIPs.

:::trap
"Smart beta", "momentum", "quality" and other factor index funds are rule-based but not the same as a broad market index. They can lag badly for years. Start broad and simple.
:::

:::dive Is active investing ever worth it?
Some active funds do beat their index over long periods, and some categories (like smaller companies) have offered more chances to do so. But identifying them **in advance** is the hard part, and costs are certain while outperformance isn't. A reasonable approach many follow is an index-fund core, with any active funds as a smaller part chosen on cost, consistency and a clear reason. Never because of last year's chart.
:::
