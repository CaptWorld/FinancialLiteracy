:::hook
Every generation of Indian investors meets its scam. The names change; the pattern doesn't: **a rising price, a compelling story, trusted names, and nobody asking where the cash is.**
:::

## 1992: Harshad Mehta and the bank-receipt scam

In the early 1990s, banks traded government securities among themselves using instruments like **bank receipts**. The system had weak controls. A broker, Harshad Mehta, and others used fake or misused receipts to divert thousands of crores from banks into the stock market. Shares he favoured rose spectacularly, and the Sensex more than tripled in about a year.

When journalist Sucheta Dalal exposed it in April 1992, the money had to go back. Prices crashed, and banks and investors lost heavily.

**What changed:** SEBI was given statutory powers in 1992 and stronger enforcement; **NSE** brought screen-based trading (1994); the **Depositories Act (1996)** made shares electronic (demat), ending fake share certificates and delayed settlements. Many of today's protections exist because of this crisis.

## 2001: Ketan Parekh and the "K-10" stocks

A group of mostly technology and media stocks was pushed up with borrowed money, including loans from a cooperative bank. When the dot-com bubble burst globally and funding dried up, the stocks collapsed. A cooperative bank failed, and investors who'd chased the rally lost most of their money. SEBI tightened rules on trading and broker lending afterwards.

## 2009: Satyam (Hyderabad)

Satyam Computer Services was one of India's top IT firms, headquartered in Hyderabad. In January 2009, its chairman B. Ramalinga Raju wrote to the board admitting that profits had been inflated for years and that **thousands of crores of reported cash didn't exist**. The share price collapsed within days. The government stepped in, and the business was later sold to Tech Mahindra.

**Red flags in hindsight:** huge reported cash that earned surprisingly little interest; a planned acquisition of promoter-linked companies that would have used up that "cash"; and the auditor's failure, which later drew penalties.

**What changed:** stricter rules for auditors and independent directors, and more scrutiny of related-party deals (Module 9).

## The pattern, and your defences

| Pattern | Defence |
|---|---|
| Prices rising fast on a story | Ask what the cash flows say (Module 9) |
| Trusted names and brands | Diversify; no single company should be able to hurt you badly (Module 10) |
| Borrowed money fuelling the rally | Watch debt, pledges, and never borrow to invest |
| Tips and urgency | Ignore; only regulated intermediaries (Modules 10, 11) |

:::trap
"It's a big, famous company, nothing can go wrong" is precisely what Satyam's shareholders thought. Fame isn't safety. Diversification is.
:::
