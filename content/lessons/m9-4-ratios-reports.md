:::hook
Screener websites show 50 ratios for every company. Beginners either ignore them all or drown in them. **About eight ratios and three parts of the annual report tell you most of what matters.**
:::

## Deccan Chai's report card

| Ratio | Formula | Deccan Chai | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Revenue growth** | This year ÷ last year − 1 | (needs 2 years) | Is it growing? |
| **EBITDA margin** | EBITDA ÷ revenue | 100 ÷ 500 = **20%** | Operating efficiency |
| **Net margin** | PAT ÷ revenue | 45 ÷ 500 = **9%** | What's left for owners |
| **ROE** | PAT ÷ equity | 45 ÷ 240 = **18.75%** | Return on shareholders' money |
| **ROCE** | EBIT ÷ (equity + debt) | 70 ÷ 340 ≈ **20.6%** | Return on all capital |
| **Debt-to-equity** | Debt ÷ equity | 100 ÷ 240 ≈ **0.42** | Leverage |
| **Interest coverage** | EBIT ÷ interest | 70 ÷ 10 = **7×** | Can it service its debt comfortably? |
| **Free cash flow** | Op. cash flow − capex | 60 − 50 = **₹10 cr** | Real spare cash |

Consistently **high ROCE with low debt and good cash flow** is the signature of a quality business. One good year isn't.

## Valuation ratios: what the market is paying

Deccan Chai's share price is ₹90 with 10 crore shares → **market capitalisation ₹900 cr**.

- **P/E** = price ÷ EPS = 90 ÷ 4.5 = **20**
- **P/B** = price ÷ book value per share = 90 ÷ 24 = **3.75**
- **Dividend yield** = dividend per share ÷ price = ₹1 ÷ ₹90 ≈ **1.1%**

These say how **expensive** the share is relative to profits and book value, **not** how good the company is. A great company at a sky-high P/E can be a poor investment; Module 10 goes deeper.

## Ownership: promoters and pledges

Every quarter, listed companies disclose their **shareholding pattern**: promoters, mutual funds, foreign investors, public. Watch:

- **Promoter holding** falling sharply without a good reason.
- **Pledged shares**: promoters have borrowed against their shares. In a price fall, lenders can sell them, pushing the price down further. High or rising pledges are a classic red flag.

## A 30-minute annual-report routine

1. **Chairman's letter and MD&A** (Management Discussion & Analysis): the story. What do they say about growth, risks and capital allocation?
2. **Financial statements** (consolidated): fill in the table above for 3–5 years.
3. **Cash flow statement**: is operating cash flow tracking profit?
4. **Auditor's report**: any qualifications or emphasis of matter?
5. **Related-party transactions**: anything large or growing?
6. **Shareholding pattern** (from BSE/NSE): promoter holding and pledges.

:::trap
Ratios from screener sites are calculated automatically and can be wrong or inconsistent (standalone vs consolidated, one-off items). For anything important, check against the annual report itself. And be careful of "stock ideas" posted alongside screener screenshots; that's Module 10's territory.
:::

:::dive Why ROCE often matters more than profit growth
Imagine two companies each growing profit 15% a year. One needs ₹100 of new capital for every ₹15 of extra profit (ROCE 15%); the other needs only ₹40 (ROCE ~37%). The second can fund growth from its own cash and still pay dividends; the first must keep borrowing or issuing shares. Over decades, high-ROCE businesses tend to create far more value per rupee invested, which is why long-term investors watch it so closely.
:::

:::
